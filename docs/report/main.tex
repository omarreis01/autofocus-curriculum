\chapter{INTRODUCTION}
\pagenumbering{arabic}

A newborn sees the world blurry and with low contrast. Visual acuity and contrast sensitivity improve over the first months of life. One view says that this slow start is useful, not a defect: the visual system first learns coarse shape and only later fine texture, and this may make adult vision more robust to degraded input \cite{vogelsang2018}. Deep networks give a way to test this idea. Several studies trained convolutional networks on a hand-made blurry-to-sharp schedule and measured robustness \cite{vogelsang2018,jang2021blurry,arslan2024noise}.

In all of these studies a researcher writes the schedule. Our project asks one step back: \emph{if the network chooses the blur and contrast of its own training images, based only on how fast it learns, does it start blurry and faint like an infant? And does that make it more robust?}

We use a teacher from automated curriculum learning \cite{graves2017}. The teacher is a non-stationary multi-armed bandit. Each arm is one (blur, contrast) level. Before each training step the teacher picks an arm, the batch is degraded at that level, and the network takes one SGD step. The teacher's reward is learning progress on the training data only. Robustness, shape bias and test data never enter the reward. We measure them only after training. If robustness were in the reward, the teacher would optimise it directly, and we could not say that an order ``emerged''.

We test two hypotheses:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{H1 (an infant-like order emerges).} A learning-progress teacher picks blurry, low-contrast arms early in training and sharp arms late.
\item \textbf{H2 (robustness follows).} The self-chosen schedule gives more robustness and more shape bias than controls, including a control that sees the same arms in a shuffled order.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Main findings.} On CIFAR-10 with ResNet-18 and three seeds, H1 is rejected: none of the seven teacher variants we tried started blurry. With the raw learning-progress reward the teacher starts near uniform and then moves to the most degraded arm. For H2, robustness and shape bias follow \emph{exposure to blur}, not order. At 100 epochs, no ordered schedule beat a uniform random arm per batch on CIFAR-10-C. The direction of an order still matters: sharp-to-blurry is the worst order. Batch-level contrast training has almost no effect under BatchNorm; contrast is learned only with per-image mixing or GroupNorm.

Chapter~2 defines the project and its plan. Chapter~3 reviews related work. Chapter~4 gives the method. Chapters~5--7 describe the experiment framework as software: requirements, design, implementation and testing. Chapter~8 reports the results and how sure we are of each claim. Chapter~9 concludes.

\section{Broad Impact}

The project is basic research. Its main value is understanding. First, it tests a popular analogy between infant vision and curriculum learning with a design that separates order from exposure. A clear negative result for H1 is useful: it tells the next study that a learning-progress teacher alone does not reproduce the developmental order, and it shows why (the reward scale favours the hardest arm). Second, the results give practical guidance for robustness training: mixing blurred images throughout training was at least as good as any ordered schedule we tested, and batch-level contrast augmentation does not work with BatchNorm. Third, the code base is public and reproducible (fixed seeds, bit-exact resume, 52 tests), so other students can extend it. We do not expect a direct product. Robustness research can, in principle, help both good and bad uses of vision systems; our small CIFAR-10 study adds almost nothing to that risk.

\section{Ethical Considerations}

We use only public benchmark data: CIFAR-10 \cite{krizhevsky2009} and CIFAR-10-C \cite{hendrycks2019}. There are no human subjects, no personal data and no data collection. The models are small image classifiers for ten object classes and are not deployed. The main cost is compute and the energy it uses: all runs together used a few hours of one cloud A100 GPU within a 300-compute-unit budget, and we kept runs short (30 and 100 epochs) and parallel to limit this. We report negative results and the limits of our evidence (three seeds, one dataset, one model) so that the claims are not stronger than the data. The repository is public and the advisor can inspect every step.

\section{Project Definition}

The project builds an experiment framework and uses it to answer H1 and H2. The scope is:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Data and model.} CIFAR-10 (50\,000 training and 10\,000 test images, $32\times32$ pixels) and a CIFAR-style ResNet-18 \cite{he2016resnet}.
\item \textbf{Arms.} A grid of 4 blur levels ($\sigma\in\{0, 0.75, 1.5, 3\}$ pixels, Gaussian) $\times$ 4 contrast levels ($c\in\{1, 0.6, 0.35, 0.2\}$) = 16 arms. The arm $(0,1)$ is the sharp image.
\item \textbf{Teacher.} Exp3.S \cite{graves2017,auer2002} with a learning-progress reward (prediction gain and variants), and ALP-GMM \cite{portelas2019} as a different teacher.
\item \textbf{Controls.} Sharp only, random arm per batch, a hand-made blurry-to-sharp staircase, its reverse, and the teacher's own arm log shuffled in time.
\item \textbf{Measures.} Clean test accuracy, CIFAR-10-C split into ``seen'' and ``unseen'' corruptions, a frequency-based shape-bias proxy, and PGD adversarial accuracy.
\item \textbf{Budgets.} 30 epochs (short) and 100 epochs (full), 3 seeds each.
\end{itemize}
The deliverables are the public code repository\footnote{\url{https://github.com/omarreis01/autofocus-curriculum}}, the result summaries, the experiment log, and this report.

\subsection{Success Criteria}

We set the following criteria before the experiments. All were met.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Pipeline is correct.} The sharp condition reaches about 93\% clean accuracy after 30 epochs (known value for ResNet-18). Result: 93.2\%.
\item \textbf{Teacher is sane.} With a network that does not learn, the teacher gives no reward and stays uniform. With one arm that pays more, it concentrates on that arm. Both are unit tests.
\item \textbf{Fair comparison.} Every condition uses the same number of steps, the same learning-rate schedule and the same data order. This is tested.
\item \textbf{Statistics.} Every reported number is the mean $\pm$ standard deviation over 3 seeds, at a short and a full budget.
\item \textbf{A clear answer.} The report answers H1 and H2 either way, with evidence for each claim. A negative result counts as a result.
\end{enumerate}

\chapter{RELATED WORK}

\textbf{Automated curriculum with bandits.} Graves et al.\ \cite{graves2017} let a non-stationary bandit, Exp3.S \cite{auer2002}, choose which task to sample next. The reward is learning progress, for example the prediction gain (PG): the drop in loss on a batch after one update. They found that uniform sampling over tasks is a strong baseline. They also warned that PG can favour arms with high gradient variance. Our method uses this teacher directly; the arms are image degradation levels instead of tasks. Portelas et al.\ \cite{portelas2019} proposed ALP-GMM, which fits a Gaussian mixture over a continuous task space and samples where the absolute learning progress is high. We use it as a second, different teacher.

\textbf{Low-frequency-first training.} EfficientTrain \cite{wang2023efficienttrain} and EfficientTrain++ \cite{wang2024efficienttrainpp} show the network low-frequency content first and the full image later. This speeds up ImageNet training by about 1.5--3$\times$ without accuracy loss. This is the closest prior work. The schedule is found offline and then fixed, the goal is compute, and robustness is not measured. Our hand-made staircase is a version of this fixed schedule.

\textbf{When do curricula help?} Wu, Dyer and Neyshabur \cite{wu2021curricula} trained thousands of models and found that curriculum, anti-curriculum and random order give the same result in standard training; small gains come from pacing, and curricula help mainly with short budgets or noisy labels. This is the main reason for our shuffled, random and reverse controls and for the two budgets. Hacohen and Weinshall \cite{hacohen2019} split a curriculum into a scoring function and a pacing function, and report small gains (1--2 points) from easy-to-hard order. In our design one bandit sets both order and pacing, so we keep the learning-rate schedule the same in every condition.

\textbf{Developmental vision and blur.} Vogelsang et al.\ \cite{vogelsang2018} argue that initially poor acuity may be adaptive and support this with networks trained blurry-to-sharp. Katzhendler and Weinshall \cite{katzhendler2019} replied that low-resolution images could simply be mixed in at all times; the benefit may come from exposure, not order. Jang and Tong \cite{jang2021blurry} found that for objects (unlike faces) the robustness from blurry-to-clear training is not kept at the end of training. In later work \cite{jang2024blur} they showed that training with mixed blur gives networks more shape sensitivity and more robustness to noise. Arslan, Jang and Sinha \cite{arslan2024noise} showed that decreasing internal neural noise from high to low during training, and low-to-high resolution, improves robustness to degraded input. All of these schedules are set by hand. Our question is whether the order appears when the network chooses. Contrast is our addition.

\textbf{Robustness and shape measures.} CIFAR-10-C \cite{hendrycks2019} applies 19 corruptions at 5 severities to the CIFAR-10 test set. Geirhos et al.\ \cite{geirhos2019} defined shape bias with style-transfer cue-conflict images; we use a simpler frequency proxy because that set does not fit $32\times32$ images. Wang et al.\ \cite{wang2020highfreq} showed that CNNs use high-frequency components that humans cannot see, which motivates our low-/high-pass probes. PGD \cite{madry2018} is the standard iterative adversarial attack.

\textbf{Normalisation.} BatchNorm \cite{ioffe2015} normalises with batch statistics in training and with running averages at test time. GroupNorm \cite{wu2018gn} normalises within each sample and has no batch statistics. This difference turned out to be central for contrast (Section~\ref{sec:bn}).

\chapter{METHODOLOGY}

\section{Data, Model and Training}

We train on CIFAR-10 with random crop (padding 4) and horizontal flip. Blur and contrast are applied on the GPU after augmentation. The model is ResNet-18 in its CIFAR form ($3\times3$ stem, no max-pool) with BatchNorm; one group of experiments uses GroupNorm with 32 groups. We use SGD with Nesterov momentum 0.9, weight decay $5\cdot10^{-4}$, batch size 512 and bf16 mixed precision. The learning rate rises linearly to 0.2 over the first 15\% of steps and then follows a cosine to zero. This schedule is the same in every condition. The short budget is 30 epochs (2\,910 steps) and the full budget is 100 epochs (9\,700 steps), with 97 steps per epoch. Every condition runs with seeds 0, 1 and 2.

\section{Arms: Blur and Contrast}

An arm is a pair $(\sigma, c)$. Blur is a separable Gaussian filter with standard deviation $\sigma$ pixels and reflect padding. Contrast moves every pixel toward the image mean $m$ of its channel:
\begin{equation}
x' = m + c\,(x - m).
\end{equation}
Blur is applied first, then contrast. In all conditions except one (\cond{random\_image}), every image in a batch gets the same arm.

\section{Teacher: Exp3.S with Learning-Progress Rewards}

At step $t$ the teacher keeps weights $w_i$ for the $K=16$ arms and samples arm $a_t$ from
\begin{equation}
\pi_t(i) = (1-\epsilon)\,\frac{e^{w_i}}{\sum_j e^{w_j}} + \frac{\epsilon}{K}.
\end{equation}
The batch is degraded with arm $a_t$ and the network takes one SGD step. The teacher then measures a raw reward $\tilde r_t$:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{PG (prediction gain):} loss on the same batch before the update minus loss after it. One extra forward pass.
\item \textbf{SPG:} the same difference on a fresh batch from the same arm. This removes the bias of measuring on the batch just trained on.
\item \textbf{RPG (relative PG):} PG divided by the loss before the update. This removes the scale of the loss.
\end{itemize}
The raw reward is mapped to $[-1,1]$ with the 20th and 80th percentiles $q^{lo}, q^{hi}$ of the last 1000 raw rewards \cite{graves2017}:
\begin{equation}
r_t = \operatorname{clip}\!\left(2\,\frac{\tilde r_t - q^{lo}}{q^{hi}-q^{lo}} - 1,\; -1,\; 1\right).
\end{equation}
The Exp3.S update with importance weighting is $w_{a_t} \leftarrow w_{a_t} + \eta\, r_t / \pi_t(a_t)$, followed by weight sharing with rate $\alpha_t = 1/t$ so that the teacher can track a changing best arm. We use $\eta=0.01$, $\epsilon=0.05$. Graves et al.\ use $\eta=10^{-3}$ for about $10^5$ steps; our runs have only 3k--10k steps, and with $10^{-3}$ the policy did not move. The forward passes that measure the reward do not change the BatchNorm running statistics, so a teacher run differs from a fixed-arm run only in the choice of arms.

\textbf{ALP-GMM.} The second teacher samples a continuous point in the $(\sigma, c)$ box (scaled to $[0,1]^2$), with 20\% random samples and otherwise from a Gaussian mixture fitted every 250 steps on the absolute learning progress. Each sample is rounded to the nearest of the 16 arms for training.

\section{Measures}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Clean:} CIFAR-10 test accuracy.
\item \textbf{CIFAR-10-C} \cite{hendrycks2019}: 19 corruptions $\times$ 5 severities. \emph{Seen} = the 6 corruptions close to our training degradations (Gaussian, defocus, glass, motion and zoom blur, contrast). \emph{Unseen} = the other 13 (noise, weather, digital). We also report the mean of the 4 noise corruptions and the contrast corruption alone.
\item \textbf{Shape bias (CIFAR proxy):} a hybrid image is the low-frequency part (Gaussian $\sigma=2$) of an image of class A plus the high-frequency part of an image of class B. Shape bias is the share of A answers among A or B answers, over about 10\,000 pairs. This is not Geirhos' style-transfer test \cite{geirhos2019}; it measures reliance on low versus high frequencies. We also report accuracy on high-pass images ($\sigma=1$).
\item \textbf{PGD-10} \cite{madry2018}: $L_\infty$, $\epsilon=1/255$, step $\epsilon/4$, random start, 2\,000 test images. We also measured FGSM but do not use it, because it looks gradient-masked (at 100 epochs, \cond{sharp} scores higher under FGSM at $2/255$ than under PGD at $1/255$: 53.6 vs 44.9).
\end{itemize}

\section{BatchNorm Recalibration}
\label{sec:bn}

In the pilot, \cond{sharp} was normal, but every condition that mixes arms over time scored only 13--60\% clean accuracy. The cause is BatchNorm. Each batch comes from one arm. In training, BatchNorm uses the statistics of the current batch, so this is fine. At test time it uses running averages, which are a mix of degraded batches and do not fit clean images. We tested this: after recomputing the running statistics on 100 batches of clean \emph{training} images, \cond{random} rose from 60 to 89.1\% and \cond{teacher\_spg} from 16 to 87.2\%, while \cond{sharp} did not change. We therefore recalibrate BatchNorm this way before every evaluation and probe. No test data is used.

The same mechanism acts during training. When a whole batch has contrast $c$, the first BatchNorm layer largely removes the change of scale, so the network hardly ``sees'' low contrast. This is an explanation; Section~8.5 gives the experiments that support it.

\chapter{IMPLEMENTATION AND TESTING}

\section{Implementation}

The code is Python with PyTorch, about 1\,200 lines in 14 modules in \texttt{src/} and about 340 lines of tests. The main implementation choices are:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{GPU-resident data.} The whole CIFAR-10 training set is a \texttt{uint8} tensor on the GPU. Augmentation, blur and contrast run on the GPU. There is no CPU data loader. This gives 2.0\,s per epoch on an A100.
\item \textbf{Reproducible randomness.} Each epoch uses a generator seeded by (seed, epoch) for data order and augmentation. SPG draws its fresh batch from a second, separate stream, so the training data order is the same as in every other condition.
\item \textbf{Side-effect-free reward.} \texttt{measure\_loss} runs a train-mode forward pass with \texttt{track\_running\_stats} turned off, so measuring the reward does not change BatchNorm statistics. The only GPU synchronisation per step is reading the raw reward.
\item \textbf{Resumable runs.} The checkpoint holds the model, optimiser, scaler, schedule state (including RNG states) and the arm log. It is written to a temporary file and renamed (atomic write). Metrics rows after the restored epoch are dropped.
\item \textbf{Stable bandit.} Exp3.S keeps log-weights normalised to a maximum of 0, so the exponentials do not overflow over long runs.
\item \textbf{Data download.} CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-10-C come from Hugging Face mirrors because the official servers were very slow. The CIFAR-10 archive is checked by MD5, and the CIFAR-10-C label order was checked against the official test labels.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Problems during implementation.} Three problems shaped the work. (1) The BatchNorm mismatch (Section~\ref{sec:bn}), found in the pilot and fixed by recalibration before every evaluation. (2) Slow dataset servers, solved with verified mirrors. (3) CUDA out-of-memory when 12 runs shared one GPU: three GroupNorm and per-image runs died and were re-run at 6 in parallel from their checkpoints (\texttt{colab/finish\_ext.sh}); the set-up is the same, so the results are not affected.

\section{Does the Teacher Start Blurry?}

This is the direct test of H1. Figure~\ref{fig:curves30} shows the mean blur $\sigma$ and mean contrast of the batches trained in each epoch. \section{Where Does the Teacher Go?}

Figure~\ref{fig:worst} shows the share of batches on the most degraded arm $(\sigma=3, c=0.2)$ in each fifth of training. Raw PG piles up there (71--74\% in the middle of a 30-epoch run, up to 83\% at 100 epochs). RPG and ALP-GMM stay at the uniform level (1/16).

\textbf{Why the hardest arm?} Raw PG is a drop in loss. The loss is highest on the most degraded arm, so one SGD step lowers it most there, and the raw reward is largest. RPG divides by the loss and removes this scale. Then the share of the hardest arm falls from 71--74\% (PG) to 1--3\% (RPG). The RPG experiment supports this explanation. We have not yet analysed the per-arm reward distribution directly (the rewards are stored in \texttt{teacher.npz}).

\section{Three Orders: Blurry-to-Sharp, Sharp-to-Blurry, Random}

\chapter{CONCLUSION}

We asked whether a network that chooses its own blur and contrast, rewarded only by learning progress, starts blurry like an infant, and whether this makes it more robust.

\textbf{H1 is rejected in our set-up.} No teacher variant started blurry. With raw prediction gain the teacher moves to the most degraded arm, because the loss drop is largest there; relative progress and ALP-GMM stay near uniform. A learning-progress reward alone does not reproduce the developmental order.

\textbf{H2: exposure, not order.} Robustness to corruptions, PGD robustness and the shape-bias proxy grow with exposure to blur. A uniform random arm per batch was the best of the seven main conditions on CIFAR-10-C at 100 epochs. The teacher's order adds no measurable robustness over the same arms shuffled. Direction still matters: sharp-to-blurry is the worst order. Contrast training works only per image or without BatchNorm.

These results agree with earlier warnings that curriculum gains often come from exposure \cite{wu2021curricula,katzhendler2019} and that mixed blur is a strong baseline \cite{jang2024blur}.

\textbf{Limitations.}
\begin{itemize}
\item One dataset (CIFAR-10, 32 pixels) and one model (ResNet-18). At 32 pixels, $\sigma=3$ blur is very strong.
\item Three seeds. Differences below 1 point are weak evidence.
\item The shape-bias measure is a frequency proxy, not Geirhos' style-transfer test.
\item BatchNorm recalibration is a choice. Another fix (for example mixed batches during training) may give different clean numbers.
\item ALP-GMM samples a continuous space but training rounds to the 16 arms; a continuous version was not tried.
\item The \cond{random\_image} arm log stores only the first image's arm (an unbiased but incomplete histogram).
\item We did not run the noise tests of Arslan et al.\ \cite{arslan2024noise}; the CIFAR-10-C noise corruptions are only a close match.
\item The 13 extended conditions ran only at 30 epochs.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Future work.}
\begin{itemize}
\item Run \cond{random\_image}, \cond{random\_blur}, \cond{teacher\_rpg} and \cond{teacher\_alp} at 100 epochs (about 40 minutes on an A100) to test ``random is best'' against them.
\item A per-image teacher that chooses a distribution over arms for each batch, which would combine the contrast benefit of per-image mixing with a teacher.
\item A transfer-progress reward: the loss drop on a clean (sharp) validation batch. This steers the teacher to the arm that helps sharp vision most, not to the arm where loss falls most. Robustness would still stay out of the reward.
\item A per-arm reward analysis from \texttt{teacher.npz}, to test the hard-first explanation directly.
\item A larger dataset (for example Tiny-ImageNet), if the budget allows.
\end{itemize}
