## 1. Main claim

The paper asks if ordering training examples by difficulty helps. Examples are learned in a very consistent order ("implicit curricula"). On standard benchmarks, curriculum, anti-curriculum and random ordering all give about the same accuracy, so ordering adds almost nothing. Curriculum (easy to hard), but not anti-curriculum, helps when training time is short or labels are noisy.

Location: Abstract; Contributions list (Section 1); Discussion.

Three orderings (Section 3, Algorithm 1):
- Curriculum: easiest first.
- Anti-curriculum: hardest first.
- Random: random order inside the growing subset. This is NOT standard i.i.d. training, because the pacing function still changes the dataset size over time. This is the paper's control for pacing.

Difficulty scores (Section 3.1, Appendix A):
- Loss of a reference model.
- Learned iteration (first epoch after which the model stays correct on that example).
- Estimated c-score (Jiang et al. 2020, held-out consistency). A loss-based c-score is also used.
- The three scores correlate strongly (Figure 3, Figure 12). All sweeps after 3.1 use the c-score. Exact c-score variant for the sweeps: NOT CONFIRMED.
- The ordering pass uses a clean, non-augmented data loader (Appendix B).

Pacing functions (Section 3.2, table; Figure 4, Figure 14):
- Six shapes: logarithmic, exponential, step, linear, quadratic, root (polynomial with p = 1/2, 1, 2).
- a = fraction of training until the full dataset is in use. b = starting fraction of the data. If a = 0 or b = 1 the schedule equals standard training.
- Grid: a in {0.01, 0.1, 0.2, 0.4, 0.8, 1.6}; b in {0.0025, 0.1, 0.2, 0.4, 0.8}. That gives 180 pacing functions.
- Inconsistency: the Figure 14 caption lists b up to 1.0. The Section 3.2 grid does not. Treat the exact grid as NOT CONFIRMED.

## 4. Conclusion (Discussion section)

- Implicit curricula exist: examples are learned in a consistent order.
- Curricula do not help in standard training.
- Easy-to-hard ordering can help under a limited time budget or with noisy labels.
- Limits: over 25,000 models, but compute forced a trade-off. The authors chose many orderings and pacing functions over many tasks, scales and models. They warn against generalizing to tasks or regimes unlike those studied.

## 5. Implicit curricula (Section 2, Figure 2; Section 3.2, Figure 3)

- "Learned iteration" = the epoch from which the model predicts the example correctly at that epoch and all later epochs.
- The learned order is consistent across seeds and across similar architectures. Conv nets agree with each other more than with fully connected nets. Figure 2 right shows high Spearman correlations inside architecture families. No numeric Spearman values in the text.
- So "difficulty" is largely a property of the data, not of one model.
- Explicit curricula change the learned order only a little, except anti-curriculum, which forces hard examples earlier (Section 3.2, Figure 3).
- One outlier: ResNet-18 training loss at epoch 180 stops correlating with the other scores. The authors suggest memorization (Section 3.1).

## 6. When curricula DID help

Limited training budget (Section 5, "Limited training time budget"; Figures 6, 16, 19, 20):
- Budgets tested: T = 17,600, 1,760, 352 steps. By my arithmetic that is about 50, 5 and 1 epochs. The text gives no epoch counts.
- Cosine LR decays to zero over T steps. Appendix B says short runs thus "accelerate" the LR decrease. So the short-budget comparison includes a faster LR decay.
- At 17,600 steps: curricula are no better than standard.
- At 1,760 and 352 steps: curriculum helps; the gain grows as the budget shrinks. Anti-curriculum does not help.
- At 352 steps: random ordering also beats the baseline (see item 7).
- Many of the best pacing functions start with a small subset and keep it small for much of training (Figure 8, middle panels).
- Conflicts in the text: Section 5 text and Appendix B disagree about the baseline step count (17,600 vs 35,200; the HTML also shows a duplicated "1760017600" that looks like a rendering error). The Figure 16 caption (CIFAR-10) gives 19,550 / 3,910 / 391 steps. Reason NOT CONFIRMED.
- FOOD-101 (Section 6, Figure 10): standard time (29,600 steps, about 100 epochs) shows no benefit. Short time (1,480 steps, about 5 epochs) shows a curriculum benefit.
- No accuracy numbers in the text.

Noisy labels (Section 5, "Data with noisy labels"; Figures 7, 8, 9, 17, 21):
- CIFAR-100 only. Noise at 20%, 40%, 60%, 80%, made by randomly permuting labels. The c-score is recomputed on the noisy data. Training length: 35,200 steps (Figure 21 caption).
- Curriculum beats the other orderings by a large margin at all noise levels tested (the paper's wording, Section 5).
- Best pacing functions at 20% and 60% noise (step, exponential) effectively ignore the noisy data. The 40% case was not covered by that sweep grid.
- Explanation (Figure 9): label noise and short training both shift the c-score distribution toward harder examples. So starting with the easy examples helps.
- FOOD-101N (310k noisy images, 121,100 steps, single seed): curriculum helps (Section 6, Figure 10). One seed only, so weak evidence.
- No accuracy numbers in the text.

## 7. Ordering or pacing?

Standard training (Section 4, "Marginal value of ordered learning"; Figure 5):
- No pacing function and no ordering significantly beats extra standard SGD runs. The best CIFAR-10 mean came from random ordering. The best single CIFAR-100 run was also random.
- Verdict, exact words: "any marginal benefit of ordered learning is due entirely to the dynamic dataset size"
- So under standard training the benefit (if any) comes from PACING, not ORDERING.
- Baselines (Figure 5): standard1 = mean of all 540 standard runs; standard2 = best of 180 groups of 3 runs (matches the tuning effort of the 30 (a,b) pairs x 3 seeds); standard3 = mean of top three. No named statistical test is given.
- Many pacing functions beat standard1, none significantly beat standard2. The count is NOT CONFIRMED.

Short budget (Section 5): random ordering at 352 steps beats the baseline. The authors read this as the pacing function alone helping. But curriculum beats random and anti-curriculum at 1,760 and 352 steps and under noise (Section 5, Figure 8). So ORDERING matters in those two regimes.

The paper does not explain WHY dynamic dataset size helps. No mechanism is given (Section 4/5).

## 8. Robustness, distribution shift, blur, contrast, input transforms

- Robustness to corruptions: NOT TESTED.
- Distribution shift (test set different from train): NOT TESTED.
- Blur, contrast, noise or any other input transform as a curriculum axis: NOT TESTED.
- The only corruption studied is label noise (Section 5). Evaluation is on clean test/val data.
- Augmentation is fixed: random horizontal flips (CIFAR); resize 250, random crop 224, flips (FOOD-101; Section 6, Appendix B). It is not scheduled.
- The word "robust" in the paper refers to stable conclusions across pacing choices, not to model robustness.

## 9. Example order vs. transform level

What the paper says that touches our setup:
- Section 3 (paragraph "We stress that the scoring and pacing function paradigm..."): the framework only orders a fixed dataset. It explicitly leaves out changes to the training procedure itself, with gradually increasing image resolution (Vogelsang et al. 2018) and gradually introduced classes (Weinshall et al. 2018) as examples. The bibliography title for Vogelsang et al. is about high initial visual acuity, which is the infant-vision line of work we build on. So transform-level curricula are named as out of scope.
- The same paragraph says the framework cannot do self-paced, training-dependent curricula: "cannot implement a self-paced and training-dependent curriculum" (quote). Our bandit is online and training-dependent (reward = learning progress). So the paper does not test our kind of curriculum either. Appendix A mentions self-paced and dynamic orderings only as related work.
- Nothing else applies directly. The paper has no result on blur or contrast schedules.

What carries over only by analogy (my inference, not the paper's claim):
- A transform curriculum does not change dataset size. So the pacing confound (dynamic dataset size) does not exist in the same form. But an analogous confound exists: blur and contrast change act as augmentation, so any gain could come from exposure to a blur/contrast distribution, not from its order. The paper's "random" arm is the model for a control: same exposure, no useful order.
- The paper's short-budget and noisy-data results suggest that any benefit may show up mostly when training is short or data is hard. That is a hypothesis to test, not a finding for transforms.

## 10. Three design rules for our experiments

1. Include ordering-removal controls, not only a "no curriculum" baseline.
   - Random-schedule control: sample blur/contrast levels at random with the SAME marginal distribution as the bandit chose (for example, shuffle the bandit's own schedule in time). This is the analog of the paper's random-curriculum arm. If bandit = shuffled-bandit, the order did nothing and exposure is the cause.
   - Anti-curriculum control: a sharp-first / high-contrast-first schedule. The paper shows anti-curriculum fails to help where curriculum helps, so it separates "easy first" from "any change".
   - Also keep: fixed sharp (no change), fixed blur augmentation (uniform random blur/contrast each batch), and a hand-made blurry-to-sharp schedule. These show whether the bandit adds anything over a simple schedule.

2. Equal compute, tuned baselines, several seeds, and report the spread.
   - Same total steps, same optimizer and LR schedule (tied to total steps; the paper notes that shorter runs speed up the cosine decay) for every arm.
   - At least 3 seeds per arm (the paper uses 3 and still reports noise as a limit). Compare each arm against the spread of the baseline runs, and against a best-of-N baseline that gets the same tuning effort as the bandit's hyperparameters (the paper's standard2 idea). Do not compare the best bandit run to the mean baseline.
   - Keep the bandit's reward restricted to learning progress, as planned. Measure robustness only at the end, on a fixed corruption set, for all arms.

3. Run both a full budget and a short budget, and plan for a null at full budget.
   - The paper finds no benefit at full budget (about 100 epochs) and a benefit only at about 5 epochs or fewer, or under heavy label noise. Use at least one full-length and one short run length (for example about 100 and about 5 epochs on CIFAR). Report both.
   - State before running that a null result at full budget is the expected outcome under the skeptical reading. Do not tune the setup until the bandit wins.
   - Because robustness is not in this paper at all, a robustness gain is our own new claim. It needs its own controls: compare against the fixed-blur-augmentation arm from rule 1 on the same corruptions, so we can tell "order" from "exposure to blur".
   - Optional: one label-noise run (for example 20-40% on CIFAR-100) as the paper's second regime.
